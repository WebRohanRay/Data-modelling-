Yes. Let’s do this as a **real Data Engineering design case study**, not as a list of definitions.

The goal is that after this, when someone gives you a business requirement, you can think:

> **What business process is this? → What is the grain? → What is the fact? → What are the dimensions? → Do I need one star or multiple stars? → What should be shared? → What history must be preserved? → What architecture should this live in?**

That is the level of thinking you want.

Current Microsoft Fabric guidance still recommends dimensional/star-schema modeling for analytical workloads, and current Databricks guidance explicitly puts fact/dimension star schemas naturally in the Gold layer of a lakehouse. So these are not obsolete textbook ideas. ([Microsoft Learn][1])

# PART 1 — First understand the entire picture

Imagine a huge company:

# **FLASHCART**

A global quick-commerce company.

It has:

```text
Customers
Products
Stores
Warehouses
Orders
Payments
Deliveries
Inventory
Returns
Promotions
Couriers
Suppliers
Support tickets
```

Millions or billions of events happen every day.

The company wants to answer:

```text
SALES
- How much did we sell?
- What products sell best?
- Which countries generate revenue?
- Which customers generate the most revenue?

CUSTOMERS
- How many active customers?
- Which customer segments are growing?
- What is customer lifetime value?

PAYMENTS
- Which payment methods fail?
- How much money was collected?
- How many payment attempts happen per order?

DELIVERY
- How long do deliveries take?
- Which stores are late?
- Which couriers perform best?

INVENTORY
- How much stock did we have?
- Which products go out of stock?
- Which stores have excess inventory?

RETURNS
- Which products are returned?
- Why are products returned?
- How much revenue is refunded?

PROMOTIONS
- Which campaigns increase sales?
- How much discount was given?
```

Now pause.

Do you see the important thing?

**These are not one business process.**

They are:

```text
Sales
Payment
Delivery
Inventory
Return
Promotion
Customer activity
```

That observation drives your fact-table design.

Kimball's current reference material still starts dimensional design from **business processes**, because choosing the process gives you the design target from which grain, dimensions, and facts are defined. ([Kimball Group][2])

# PART 2 — The first mistake beginners make

A beginner thinks:

> "The company needs analytics, so I'll make one giant Fact table."

Maybe:

```text
FactEverything
────────────────────────────
order_id
customer_id
product_id
payment_id
delivery_id
inventory_id
return_id
sales_amount
payment_amount
delivery_time
inventory_quantity
refund_amount
discount_amount
...
```

This looks like one big solution.

It is actually a disaster.

Why?

Because these things happen at **different grains**.

For example:

```text
Sale
    = one order line

Payment
    = one payment attempt

Delivery
    = one delivery

Inventory
    = one product + store + day

Return
    = one returned order line
```

Those are different business events.

Putting them together creates:

* duplicated data
* ambiguous meaning
* incorrect sums
* massive NULL areas
* difficult pipelines
* difficult maintenance

Microsoft's current fact-table guidance also emphasizes that a fact table needs a consistent grain, and explicitly warns about mixing different levels of detail. ([Microsoft Learn][3])

So the first major rule:

# **Different business processes / incompatible grains → separate fact tables.**

---

# PART 3 — What exactly is a FACT?

Don't memorize:

> "Fact table contains numbers."

That's too weak.

A better definition:

> **A fact table represents a measurable business event, activity, or state at a clearly defined grain.**

Microsoft currently describes fact tables as storing measurements associated with observations/events such as sales or stock balances, together with dimension keys that define their dimensional context. ([Microsoft Learn][3])

Think:

```text
FACT
=
WHAT HAPPENED?
OR
WHAT STATE DID WE OBSERVE?
```

Examples:

```text
Sale
Payment
Shipment
Return
Inventory balance
Website click
Bank transaction
```

---

# PART 4 — What exactly is a DIMENSION?

A dimension describes the **context** around a fact.

Think:

```text
FACT
= what happened

DIMENSIONS
= who / what / where / when / which category
```

For a sale:

```text
Who?
→ Customer

What?
→ Product

Where?
→ Store

When?
→ Date

Which campaign?
→ Promotion
```

So:

```text
                DimCustomer
                     |
                     |
DimProduct ---- FactSales ---- DimDate
                     |
                     |
                 DimStore
                     |
                     |
               DimPromotion
```

Microsoft's current dimensional-modeling guidance uses exactly this conceptual split: dimensions describe business entities/context, while facts store measurements/events. ([Microsoft Learn][1])

---

# PART 5 — The MOST IMPORTANT concept: GRAIN

This is the thing I want permanently in your brain.

# **Grain = What does one row represent?**

Before creating a fact table, write:

> **One row = __________**

You must be able to fill that sentence.

For FlashCart sales, possible grains are:

```text
One row = one order
```

or:

```text
One row = one order line
```

or:

```text
One row = one customer per day
```

or:

```text
One row = one store-product-day
```

These are completely different models.

---

# PART 6 — Why grain changes everything

Suppose one order contains:

```text
Order
 ├── Product A
 ├── Product B
 └── Product C
```

If your grain is:

```text
one row = one order
```

you cannot naturally answer detailed questions like:

> How many units of Product A were sold?

If your grain is:

```text
one row = one order line
```

you can.

So:

```text
BUSINESS QUESTION
       ↓
NEEDED DETAIL
       ↓
GRAIN
       ↓
FACT DESIGN
```

This is why you should **never start with the column list**.

Start with the business question.

Microsoft's current fact guidance calls declaring the grain the first and most important fact-table design step. ([Microsoft Learn][3])

# PART 9 — Our first STAR SCHEMA

Now put it together.

```text
                         DimCustomer
                              |
                              |
                              |
DimProduct ------------- FactSales ------------- DimDate
                              |
                              |
                         DimStore
                              |
                              |
                        DimPromotion
```

That is a **star schema**.

Why is it called a star?

Because:

```text
                DIM
                 |
DIM ----------- FACT ----------- DIM
                 |
                DIM
```

The fact sits in the center.

Microsoft's current Fabric documentation still describes star schema as a recommended analytical modeling approach, and explicitly notes that one warehouse commonly contains multiple fact tables and therefore multiple stars. ([Microsoft Learn][1])

# PART 10 — Why fact in center?

Because most analytical questions look like:

> Give me a metric **by some dimensions**.

For example:

```text
Revenue
BY
country
BY
product category
BY
month
```

The fact supplies:

```text
revenue
```

The dimensions supply:

```text
country
product category
month
```

So the query logically becomes:

```text
FactSales
    ↓
join/filter
    ↓
DimCustomer
DimProduct
DimDate
    ↓
aggregate
    ↓
answer
```

This is why dimensional modeling is so useful for analytics.

# PART 11 — Measures vs dimensions

This distinction must become automatic.

Suppose you have:

```text
Country
Product
Category
Month
Revenue
Quantity
Profit
```

Then:

### Dimensions

```text
Country
Product
Category
Month
```

### Measures

```text
Revenue
Quantity
Profit
```

A simple rule:

> **Dimension = something you group/filter by**

> **Measure = something you calculate/summarize**

Example:

```sql
SUM(revenue)
GROUP BY country
```

`revenue` is a measure.

`country` is a dimension attribute.

---

# PART 12 — Additive, semi-additive, non-additive facts

This is where your knowledge starts becoming more advanced.

## Additive

Can be summed across all relevant dimensions.

Examples:

```text
sales_amount
quantity
cost
discount_amount
```

For example:

```text
SUM(sales_amount)
```

usually makes sense across:

```text
customer
product
store
date
```

---

## Semi-additive

Can be summed across some dimensions but not all.

Classic example:

```text
bank_account_balance
inventory_balance
```

You can perhaps sum balances across:

```text
customers
```

but summing the same balance across time can be meaningless.

If:

```text
Monday balance = 100
Tuesday balance = 120
Wednesday balance = 90
```

then:

```text
100 + 120 + 90
```

doesn't mean "Wednesday inventory."

Instead you might use:

```text
LAST_VALUE
AVERAGE
MIN
MAX
```

depending on the business question.

---

## Non-additive

Cannot meaningfully be summed.

Examples:

```text
percentage
ratio
unit price
conversion rate
```

For example:

```text
conversion_rate
```

You usually don't:

```text
SUM(conversion_rate)
```

You calculate it from appropriate underlying measures.

### Senior-level thought

Don't ask:

> "Is this column numeric?"

Ask:

> **"How is this measure mathematically valid to aggregate?"**

# PART 14 — Why FactSales and FactPayment must stay separate

Imagine:

```text
Order
 ├── Product A
 ├── Product B
 ├── Product C
 └── Product D
```

and the payment process:

```text
Payment attempt 1 → Failed
Payment attempt 2 → Failed
Payment attempt 3 → Successful
```

If you combine them:

```text
4 order lines × 3 payment attempts
```

You can produce a multiplication effect.

You may accidentally make the business think:

> "Revenue is three times larger."

This is called a **grain mismatch / fanout problem**.

This is one of the reasons a senior engineer asks for the grain before writing a query.

# PART 16 — Shared dimensions

But notice:

```text
FactSales → DimCustomer
FactPayment → DimCustomer
```

Could both use the same customer dimension?

# Yes.

That's a:

# **CONFORMED DIMENSION**

Example:

```text
                       DimCustomer
                     /            \
                    /              \
                   ↓                ↓
              FactSales       FactPayment
```

Similarly:

```text
                       DimDate
                     /        \
                    /          \
                   ↓            ↓
              FactSales      FactPayment
```

Microsoft's current guidance specifically describes conformed dimensions as shared dimensions reused across multiple fact tables/stars and highlights Date and Product as common examples. ([Microsoft Learn][4])

---

# PART 17 — Why shared dimensions matter

Suppose Sales says:

```text
Country = India
```

and Payments uses a completely different definition of country.

Now management asks:

> "Compare sales and payment activity by country."

You can get inconsistent answers.

A conformed dimension gives the organization a shared analytical definition.

Think:

```text
ONE Customer Definition
ONE Product Definition
ONE Date Definition
```

shared across business processes.

This is one of the most important enterprise-level modeling ideas.

# PART 18 — The BUS MATRIX

Now we're getting into serious warehouse architecture.

Create a matrix:

| Business Process | Customer | Product | Store | Date | Promotion |
| ---------------- | -------- | ------- | ----- | ---- | --------- |
| Sales            | ✓        | ✓       | ✓     | ✓    | ✓         |
| Payment          | ✓        | —       | ✓     | ✓    | —         |
| Delivery         | ✓        | —       | ✓     | ✓    | —         |
| Inventory        | —        | ✓       | ✓     | ✓    | —         |
| Returns          | ✓        | ✓       | ✓     | ✓    | —         |

Think about what this shows.

Rows:

```text

```

```text

```

This lets you see which dimensions are shared and should be standardized.

Kimball's current reference material still calls the enterprise bus matrix an essential planning/communication tool: rows are business processes and columns are dimensions, with shared dimensions becoming conformed. ([Kimball Group][5])

# PART 20 — Inventory is different

Requirement:

> "Management needs to know how much stock each product had in each store every day."

Ask the most important question:

### What does one row represent?

```text
ONE ROW = ONE PRODUCT + ONE STORE + ONE DAY
```

This is not a normal transaction.

It is a **state snapshot**.

So:

```text
FactInventorySnapshot
────────────────────────────
date_key
product_key
store_key

opening_quantity
received_quantity
sold_quantity
returned_quantity

```

This is a:

# **Periodic Snapshot Fact**

---

# PART 21 — Why inventory isn't just another sales fact

Because:

```text
Sale = event
Inventory = state
```

A sale says:

> something happened.

An inventory snapshot says:

> this is the observed state at this point in time.

That difference determines the fact type.

# PART 23 — Factless fact

Now something surprising.

A fact table doesn't always need numeric measures.

Example:

No obvious numeric measure.

# PART 26 — How do I decide whether to make a NEW fact?

Use this decision process.

## Question 1

### Is this a different business process?

```text
Sales
vs
Payment
```

Yes.

Likely separate facts.

---

### Question 2

### Is the grain different?

```text
Sales:
one order line

Inventory:
one product-store-day
```

Different.

Separate facts.

---

### Question 3

### Can the measures coexist at the same grain?

If yes, they can often belong in the same fact.

Example:

```text
quantity
gross_amount
discount
tax
net_amount
cost
profit
```

All are valid at:

```text
one order line
```

So:

```text
FactSales
```

is appropriate.

---

### Question 4

### Are you creating a fact only because of a different question?

Be careful.

Suppose:

> Revenue by country

and:

> Revenue by product

Those are NOT two fact tables.

Same business process.

Same grain.

Same measures.

One `FactSales` can answer both.

# PART 27 — VERY IMPORTANT: Question ≠ Fact Table

This is crucial.

Business questions:

```text
Revenue by country
Revenue by product
Revenue by customer
Revenue by month
Revenue by store
```

All can use:

```text
FactSales
```

You don't create:

```text
FactSalesByCountry
FactSalesByProduct
FactSalesByCustomer
FactSalesByMonth
```

That would be terrible design.

Instead:

```text
FactSales
   |
   +── DimCustomer
   +── DimProduct
   +── DimStore
   +── DimDate
```

Dimensions give you different analysis perspectives.

# PART 28 — When WOULD you create another fact for the same broad business area?

Now you're getting into advanced design.

Suppose Sales has:

### Detailed transaction fact

```text
FactSales
grain = one order line
```

But management runs a report thousands of times per hour:

```text
Daily sales by store and product category
```

You might create:

```text
FactSalesDailyAggregate
```

with a different grain:

```text
ONE ROW = ONE STORE + PRODUCT CATEGORY + DAY
```

That's an **aggregate fact**.

It exists for performance or specialized consumption.

Microsoft's current fact-table guidance explicitly recognizes aggregate fact tables as rollups designed to accelerate common queries. ([Microsoft Learn][3])

The key:

> **This is not because the question is different. It's because the required data grain/performance/use case is different.**

---

# PART 29 — Dimensions aren't always tiny

Another beginner mistake:

> "Fact = huge, Dimension = small."

Usually facts are very large and dimensions often have fewer rows, but dimensions can also be very large and wide.

A customer dimension can contain millions of customers and many attributes.

Microsoft's current guidance explicitly notes that dimensions can be millions of rows and potentially hundreds of attributes; what matters is whether the dimension supports the necessary filtering, grouping, and historical analysis. ([Microsoft Learn][4])

So:

> **Dimension is defined by role, not row count.**

# PART 30 — Surrogate keys

Now let's solve history.

Suppose:

```text
customer_id
```

comes from the source system.

You could put that directly into your fact.

But warehouses often create:

```text
customer_key
```

as a surrogate key.

Dimension:

```text
DimCustomer
────────────────────────────
customer_key
customer_id
customer_name
country
customer_segment
effective_from
effective_to
is_current
```

Here:

```text
customer_id
=
business/source key

customer_key
=
warehouse surrogate key
```

# PART 31 — Why surrogate keys matter

Suppose customer 101 changes country.

Old:

```text
customer_id = 101
country = India
```

New:

```text
customer_id = 101
country = Philippines
```

The business ID did not change.

But the **historical state** did.

With SCD Type 2:

```text
customer_key 10101
customer_id 101
country India
```

and later:

```text
customer_key 10102
customer_id 101
country Philippines
```

Now facts can point to the correct historical version.

# PART 32 — Slowly Changing Dimensions

This is essential.

## Type 0

Never change it.

```text
Original value stays forever
```

Memory:

> **0 = freeze**

---

## Type 1

Overwrite.

```text
India
   ↓
Philippines
```

India disappears.

Memory:

> **1 = overwrite**

---

## Type 2

Create a new version.

```text
India        ← old version
Philippines  ← current version
```

Memory:

> **2 = preserve history**

This is the one you should know deeply.

Databricks' current Lakeflow dimensional-modeling guidance explicitly supports SCD Type 2 for Gold-layer dimensions when historical tracking is required. ([Databricks Documentation][6])

---

# PART 33 — Why NOT every dimension attribute should use SCD2

This is important.

Suppose:

```text
customer_name
customer_segment
country
email
```

change.

Do you need historical versions of all of them?

Not necessarily.

The business decides.

Example:

### Name typo

```text
Rohn
↓
Rohan
```

Maybe Type 1.

### Customer segment

```text
Silver
↓
Gold
```

Maybe Type 2 because analysts want historical segmentation.

Senior thinking:

> **SCD strategy is driven by business meaning and historical requirements, not by a blanket rule.**

# PART 39 — Star vs Snowflake schema

Star:

```text
             DimProduct
                  |
                  |
DimCustomer --- FactSales --- DimDate
                  |
               DimStore
```

Snowflake:

```text
             DimCategory
                  |
             DimProduct
                  |
DimCustomer --- FactSales --- DimDate
                  |
               DimStore
```

Snowflake normalizes parts of the dimension.

### Star

Usually:

* simpler
* easier for BI
* fewer joins
* more denormalized

### Snowflake

Usually:

* more normalized
* more tables
* more joins

Don't memorize:

> "Star is always better."

Instead:

> **Choose based on workload, usability, governance, performance and platform behavior.**

Microsoft currently recommends star schema for Fabric analytical workloads, but modern cloud systems can use variations when the workload calls for them. ([Microsoft Learn][1])

Now connect this with what you learned earlier.

A transactional application might look like:

```text

   ↓

   ↓
```

Why?

Because the application needs:

Why?

Because analysts want:

```text
compare
drill down
```

So:

```text

→ optimized for operating the business

OLAP
→ optimized for analyzing the business
```

That distinction is foundational.

## Mistake 1 — No grain

```text

```

but nobody knows what one row means.

**Never do this.**

---

## Mistake 2 — Mixing grains

```text

+
payment attempt
+
delivery
```

in one table.

Bad.

---

## Mistake 3 — One fact for every business question

Bad:


when the same atomic FactSales can answer all three.

## Mistake 4 — One giant fact

```text

```

Usually a warning sign.

---

## Mistake 5 — Duplicating dimensions unnecessarily

```text

DimCustomerPayment
DimCustomerReturn
```

when all should use the same conformed customer definition.

---

## Mistake 6 — Storing descriptive attributes everywhere

Putting:

```text

customer_country
product_name
category
store_city
```

into every massive fact can create unnecessary duplication.

---

## Mistake 7 — Blindly using SCD2

Not every attribute needs historical versioning.

---

## Mistake 8 — Thinking every numeric column is a measure

An ID can be numeric.

That doesn't make it a business measure.

---

## Mistake 9 — Summing semi-additive metrics

For example, inventory balance across dates.

---

## Mistake 10 — Designing from available columns instead of business questions

The source gives you:

```text
20 columns
```

That doesn't mean the warehouse should have exactly the same 20-column structure.

# PART 54 — Now connect this to SQL

This is why your SQL learning matters.

Suppose:

```text
FactSales
DimCustomer
DimProduct
DimDate
```

You can answer:

> Revenue by country and category.

Conceptually:

```text
FactSales
   ↓
DimCustomer → country
   ↓
DimProduct → category
   ↓
SUM(net_amount)
```

SQL:

```sql
SELECT
    c.country,
    p.category,
    SUM(f.net_amount) AS revenue
FROM FactSales f
JOIN DimCustomer c
    ON f.customer_key = c.customer_key
JOIN DimProduct p
    ON f.product_key = p.product_key
GROUP BY
    c.country,
    p.category;
```

Now your SQL JOIN has meaning.

You aren't just learning:

> "How to write JOIN."

You're learning:

> **Why the model requires the JOIN.**

That's exactly how your SQL and Data Modeling should reinforce each other.

```text
BUSINESS REQUIREMENTS
        ↓
BUSINESS PROCESSES
        ↓
BUSINESS QUESTIONS / ANALYTICAL NEEDS
        ↓
GRAIN
        ↓
FACT TABLES
        ↓
DIMENSIONS
        ↓
STAR SCHEMA
        ↓
ETL / ELT
        ↓
DATA QUALITY
        ↓
PERFORMANCE / SERVING
```

### Why requirements come first

Imagine the business tells you:

> "We want to know daily inventory levels for every product in every store."

From that requirement, you discover:

```text
Business process
→ Inventory

What is being measured?
→ Inventory state

Grain
→ One product + one store + one day

Fact type
→ Periodic snapshot

Fact
→ FactInventorySnapshot

Dimensions
→ DimProduct
→ DimStore
→ DimDate
```

But suppose the requirement instead says:

> "We want to analyze every payment attempt and determine why payments fail."

Now:

```text
Business process
→ Payment

Grain
→ One payment attempt

Fact
→ FactPayment

Dimensions
→ Customer
→ Payment Method
→ Date
→ Store
```

Completely different model.

### The high-level idea

**Requirements tell you what the business needs.**

**Data modeling converts those needs into a structure.**

**Data Engineering builds the pipelines that populate that structure.**

So when designing a warehouse, your first question should always be:

> **"What does the business need to know or analyze?"**

Not:

> "What tables should I create?"

That is one of the biggest mindset differences between a beginner and a strong Data Engineer.

For our FlashCart exercise, we should therefore start **from a realistic requirements document**, identify all the business processes and analytical questions, and only then design the facts, dimensions, grains, and star schemas.
Yes. The part you're struggling with is **not understanding fact tables themselves**. It is the step **between business requirements and deciding what tables to create**.

So let's forget the terminology for a moment.

I want you to learn one repeatable thought process:

```text
BUSINESS REQUIREMENT
        ↓
1. What is the business process?
        ↓
2. What exactly needs to be measured?
        ↓
3. What is happening / what state is being captured?
        ↓
4. What should ONE ROW represent?
        ↓
5. What FACT table represents that?
        ↓
6. What DIMENSIONS describe that fact?
        ↓
7. What MEASURES belong at that grain?
        ↓
8. What history is needed?
        ↓
9. Can another fact share these dimensions?
        ↓
10. Draw the STAR SCHEMA
```

This is the skill you need.

---

# The biggest mindset change

When you receive requirements, **do not think about tables first**.

For example, the business says:

> "We want to know how much money we make from products."

Don't immediately think:

```text
FactSales
DimProduct
DimCustomer
```

First translate the sentence into a business event:

> **A product is sold.**

Then ask:

> At what level do we want to know that sale?

Maybe:

```text
one order
```

or:

```text
one product line inside an order
```

That decision is the **grain**.

Then the fact table almost designs itself.

---

# The master design method

Whenever you get requirements, do this on paper.

## Step 1 — Highlight business nouns

Requirement:

> "Management wants to analyze sales by customer, product, store and month."

Nouns:

```text
sales
customer
product
store
month
```

But don't immediately make all of them dimensions.

---

## Step 2 — Identify the business PROCESS

Ask:

> **What is the business actually doing?**

Here:

```text
Sales
```

---

## Step 3 — Find the EVENT or STATE

Ask:

> **What happened?**

Here:

```text
A product was sold.
```

---

## Step 4 — Define the GRAIN

Ask:

> **What should one row mean?**

Maybe:

```text
ONE ROW = ONE PRODUCT LINE IN ONE ORDER
```

Now the fact has a precise meaning.

---

## Step 5 — Identify dimensions

Ask:

> Who?

```text
Customer
```

> What?

```text
Product
```

> Where?

```text
Store
```

> When?

```text
Date
```

> Under what promotion?

```text
Promotion
```

---

## Step 6 — Identify measures

Ask:

> **What numbers are produced by this event at this grain?**

For an order line:

```text
quantity
unit_price
gross_amount
discount_amount
tax_amount
net_amount
cost_amount
profit_amount
```

---

## Step 7 — Check whether everything has the same grain

If you have:

```text
sales = order line
payment = payment attempt
delivery = delivery
```

STOP.

Do not put all three into one fact.

Different grain → different facts.

---

# The easiest rule for choosing FACT tables

Remember this:

> **One business process + one grain = one logical fact table.**

Not:

> One business question = one fact table.

And not:

> One source table = one fact table.

## Why not put payment inside FactSales?

Because:

```text
FactSales grain
= one order line

FactPayment grain
= one payment attempt
```

They're not the same.

Suppose:

```text
1 order
4 product lines
3 payment attempts
```

Combining them can logically produce:

```text
4 × 3 = 12
```

combinations.

You have created artificial duplication.

That can destroy your numbers.

### Remember

> **Never mix grains just because two things belong to the same order.**

# The question you were asking:

# "After I know the business requirements, what exactly do I THINK about?"

Here is the answer.

## Question 1

# WHAT BUSINESS PROCESS?

Examples:

```text
Sales
Payment
Delivery
Inventory
Returns
Attendance
Enrollment
```

---

## Question 2

# WHAT IS THE BUSINESS EVENT OR STATE?

Examples:

```text
Sale happened
Payment attempted
Delivery completed
Inventory observed
Student attended
Account balance observed
```

---

## Question 3

# WHAT LEVEL OF DETAIL DOES THE BUSINESS NEED?

This is where you decide grain.

Examples:

```text
one order
one order line
one payment attempt
one delivery
one product-store-day
one customer-day
```

---

# Question 4

# CAN I SAY "ONE ROW = ______"?

This should become your automatic test.

If you say:

```text
ONE ROW = ?
```

and you're not sure:

# STOP.

You aren't ready to create the fact yet.

---

# Question 5

# WHAT MEASURES EXIST AT THAT GRAIN?

Example:

```text
Order-line grain:
quantity
price
discount
tax
revenue
profit
```

But:

```text
Payment-attempt grain:
payment_amount
fee
```

And:

```text
Inventory-day grain:
opening_qty
closing_qty
received_qty
```

The measures change with the grain.

---

# Question 6

# WHAT DESCRIBES THE EVENT?

Ask:

```text
WHO?
WHAT?
WHEN?
WHERE?
WHICH CATEGORY?
WHICH CAMPAIGN?
WHICH METHOD?
```

These lead you toward dimensions.

For a sale:

```text
WHO → Customer
WHAT → Product
WHEN → Date
WHERE → Store
WHICH CAMPAIGN → Promotion
```

---

# Question 7

# DO MULTIPLE FACTS NEED THE SAME DIMENSION?

Suppose:

```text
FactSales → DimCustomer

FactReturn → DimCustomer
```

Then:

```text
DimCustomer
```

can potentially be a **conformed dimension**.

# When should I create another fact?

Use this test:

```text
                SHOULD I CREATE
                 ANOTHER FACT?
                       │
                       ↓
              Different business
                  process?
                 /        \
               YES         NO
                ↓           ↓
             New fact    Different grain?
                           /       \
                         YES        NO
                          ↓          ↓
                       New fact   Probably same fact
```

That simple decision tree will prevent many mistakes.

# Here is the REALLY important distinction

You might have:

```text
Revenue by Customer
Revenue by Product
Revenue by Country
Revenue by Store
Revenue by Month
```

These are **different questions**.

But they can all use:

```text
FactSales
```

because the underlying event is the same and the grain is the same.

---

But:

```text
Revenue
Payment success rate
Delivery time
Inventory balance
Return rate
```

are different business processes/states.

They may need:

```text
FactSales
FactPayment
FactDelivery
FactInventorySnapshot
FactReturn
```

# Think about a FACT as a camera

This is a useful mental trick.

Imagine every fact table is a **camera recording one type of business activity at one level of detail**.

### FactSales camera

Records:

> Every product-line sale.

### FactPayment camera

Records:

> Every payment attempt.

### FactDelivery camera

Records:

> Every delivery.

### FactInventory camera

Records:

> Inventory state every day.

You don't point one camera at everything and expect the data to stay logically clean.

# Dimensions are the lenses/context

A fact says:

```text
Something happened.
```

Dimensions tell you:

```text
Who?
What?
When?
Where?
Which type?
Which category?
```

That's why a fact is surrounded by dimensions.

When you become very comfortable, your workflow should look like this:

```text
                 BUSINESS REQUIREMENTS
                          ↓
                  Business Questions
                          ↓
                  Business Processes
                          ↓
                    Events / States
                          ↓
                       GRAIN
                          ↓
               ┌──────────┴──────────┐
               ↓                     ↓
             FACTS              DIMENSIONS
               ↓                     ↓
          Measures                Attributes
               ↓                     ↓
          Fact Type              SCD / History
               └──────────┬──────────┘
                          ↓
                   STAR SCHEMA
                          ↓
               Multiple Business Stars
                          ↓
               Conformed Dimensions
                          ↓
                 Physical Implementation
                          ↓
                ETL / ELT / Pipelines
                          ↓
                    Data Quality
                          ↓
                    Performance
```

That is the **whole mental model**.

# Now let's go one level higher

A senior engineer doesn't only ask:

> "What tables do I create?"

They ask:

### 1. What does the business need?

### 2. What business process produces that information?

### 3. What is the atomic grain?

### 4. What history is required?

### 5. What dimensions should be shared?

### 6. What measures are mathematically valid?

### 7. What is the expected volume?

### 8. What queries will people run?

### 9. Is atomic data needed, or is an aggregate enough?

### 10. What should be stored versus calculated?

### 11. What happens when source data changes?

### 12. How will data quality be enforced?

### 13. How will this work in Bronze/Silver/Gold?

### 14. What should be optimized physically?

### 15. Who will consume the final data?

That's how you move away from "table-maker" thinking.

# One more critical concept: Source model ≠ Warehouse model

Suppose source OLTP has:

```text
Customers
Orders
OrderItems
Products
Payments
```

You should NOT automatically copy those directly into the Gold analytical model.

Source model:

```text
             Customers
                 |
               Orders
                 |
             OrderItems
                 |
              Products
```

Analytical model may become:

```text
DimCustomer
DimProduct
DimDate
DimStore
      \       |       /
        FactSales
```

Why?

Because they have different purposes.

```text
OLTP
→ run applications

Warehouse / analytical model
→ analyze the business
```

# Your final cheat sheet

When you receive **ANY** business requirement, write this:

```text
1. BUSINESS REQUIREMENT
   ↓
2. BUSINESS PROCESS
   ↓
3. EVENT OR STATE
   ↓
4. GRAIN
   "ONE ROW = __________"
   ↓
5. FACT TYPE
   Transaction?
   Periodic Snapshot?
   Accumulating Snapshot?
   Factless?
   ↓
6. FACT TABLE
   ↓
7. MEASURES
   ↓
8. DIMENSIONS
   ↓
9. DIMENSION ATTRIBUTES
   ↓
10. KEYS
    ↓
11. SCD / HISTORY
    ↓
12. CONFORMED DIMENSIONS
    ↓
13. STAR SCHEMA
    ↓
14. CHECK FOR GRAIN MISMATCH
    ↓
15. CHECK BUSINESS QUESTIONS
```

And the **three golden rules**:

> **Business process determines the fact.**

> **Grain determines what belongs inside the fact.**

> **Dimensions provide the context for analyzing the fact.**

Once those three become instinctive, **fact tables, dimensions, star schemas, multiple stars, SCDs, snapshots, conformed dimensions, and warehouse design all start fitting together naturally.**
