Yes. Let's do **one giant enterprise case study** and use it to teach the whole thing from requirements → business processes → grain → facts → dimensions → multiple stars → SCD → snapshots → factless facts → bridges → conformed dimensions → Gold layer → physical design → data quality → performance.

I’ll deliberately make it complex enough that you have to **think like a Data Engineer/warehouse architect**, not memorize textbook definitions.

Current Microsoft guidance still treats star schema, fact/dimension separation, consistent grain, surrogate keys, role-playing dimensions, SCDs, and multiple fact tables as core dimensional-modeling practices. Databricks’ current 2026 guidance also places dimensional fact/dimension models naturally in the Gold layer of a medallion/lakehouse architecture. ([Microsoft Learn][1])

# PART 3 — WHY BUSINESS PROCESS COMES FIRST

Suppose management says:

> "Show revenue by country."

You don't create:

```text
FactRevenueByCountry
```

Why?

Because that's a **question**, not necessarily a business process.

Revenue was generated by some process.

Probably:

```text
Sales
```

Then you identify:

```text
Sales → Order → Order Line
```

Then determine the grain.

So:

```text
Question
 ↓
Business process
 ↓
Event/state
 ↓
Grain
 ↓
Fact
```

This is the chain I want you to remember.

# PART 7 — WHY DID WE NOT PUT PRODUCT NAME IN FACTSALES?

```text

─────────────────────────

product_name
brand
category

```

You are repeating descriptive information billions of times.

Instead:

```text

    |
product_key
    ↓

```

And:

```text

    |
customer_key
    ↓

```

The fact represents **the event**.

Dimensions describe the event.

Current Microsoft guidance explicitly defines dimensions as descriptive entities and facts as event/measurement tables used in analytical aggregation. ([Microsoft Learn][4])

# PART 11 — WHY PAYMENT DOES NOT BELONG IN FACTSALES

This is where you're supposed to think.

Suppose one order contains:

```text
many order lines
```

and has:

```text
many payment attempts
```

Those are different grains.

If you combine:

```text
order line
+
payment attempt
```

you risk multiplication.

Therefore:

```text
FactSales
```

and:

```text
FactPayment
```

are separate.

### Key rule

> **Different grain → separate fact.**

Not always, but as a design warning, this is one of your strongest rules.

# PART 24 — WHAT DOES "CONFORMED" REALLY MEAN?

It means the dimension has a **consistent business meaning and definition across fact tables**.

Imagine:

```text
DimProduct
```

is used by:

```text
FactSales
FactReturn
FactInventory
FactPurchase
```

Then everybody uses the same:

```text
product_id
brand
category
subcategory
department
```

You avoid:

```text
SalesCategory
InventoryCategory
ReturnsCategory
```

with conflicting definitions.

Microsoft's current guidance specifically identifies conformed dimensions as dimensions shared across multiple fact tables/stars for consistency and reduced maintenance. ([Microsoft Learn][6])

# PART 26 — NOW ASK: WHY DO WE NEED DIMENSION KEYS?

Suppose `DimCustomer` contains:


```text

```

Because the warehouse may need to distinguish historical versions.

# SURROGATE KEYS

---

# PART 27 — SCD TYPE 2

Suppose a customer's segment changes.

Business says:

> "We need historical reporting."

You cannot simply overwrite:

```text
Silver → Gold
```

because then old sales would appear to have been made when the customer was Gold.

Instead create dimension versions.

Conceptually:

```text
DimCustomer
──────────────────────────────────────
customer_key
customer_id
customer_name
segment
country
effective_from
effective_to
is_current
```

Now:

```text
customer_id
   ↓
same business customer
   ↓
multiple dimension versions
```

Facts point to the correct surrogate version.

---

# PART 28 — WHY THIS MATTERS

Suppose a sale happened while customer was:

```text
Silver
```

Later:

```text
Silver → Gold
```

Historical analysis should answer:

> What was the customer classified as when the sale happened?

If you overwrote the dimension:

```text
Gold
```

you've lost the historical state.

SCD2 preserves it.

Databricks' current 2026 dimensional-modeling guidance explicitly supports SCD Type 2 in Gold-layer dimensions when historical versions are required. ([Databricks Documentation][7])

---

# PART 29 — BUT DON'T MAKE EVERY DIMENSION SCD2

This is another senior-level judgment.

Suppose:

```text
customer_name
```

contains a typo.

You probably don't need:

```text
Version 1 = Rohn
Version 2 = Rohan
```

for every typo.

You may use:

```text
SCD Type 1
```

for corrections.

But:

```text
customer_segment
sales_region
loyalty_tier
```

might require historical tracking.

Therefore:

> **History strategy is driven by business meaning.**

Not:

> "All dimensions = SCD2."

# PART 33 — BRIDGE TABLES / MANY-TO-MANY

Here's an advanced problem.

Suppose:

> One promotion can involve multiple products.

And:

> One product can belong to multiple promotions.

That's:

```text
Product M : M Promotion
```

You don't simply join them directly.

You may need a bridge:

```text
BridgeProductPromotion
────────────────────────
product_key
promotion_key
effective_from
effective_to
```

Now:

```text
DimProduct
     |
     |
BridgeProductPromotion
     |
     |
DimPromotion
```

This is useful when the relationship itself matters.

---

# PART 34 — ANOTHER MANY-TO-MANY EXAMPLE

Suppose a product can have multiple suppliers.

And a supplier can supply multiple products.

```text
Product M : M Supplier
```

Bridge:

```text
BridgeProductSupplier
────────────────────────
product_key
supplier_key
supplier_priority
effective_from
effective_to
```

Now you can answer:

> Which supplier supplied this product at a given time?

This is an example of how **business relationships** can require additional modeling structures.


Grain:

# ONE ROW = ONE ORDER FULFILLMENT LIFECYCLE

This is:

# ACCUMULATING SNAPSHOT

Because the row gets updated as the process passes through milestones.

---

# PART 37 — PERIODIC VS ACCUMULATING

Think:

### Inventory

```text
"What was the state every day?"
```

→ Periodic snapshot.

### Fulfillment

```text
"How did this order move through stages?"
```

→ Accumulating snapshot.

This distinction should become automatic.

---

# PART 38 — TRANSACTION FACT

Think:

```text
A sale happened.
A payment happened.
A return happened.
A purchase line happened.
```

These are:

# TRANSACTION FACTS

Usually the event is captured once.

Microsoft currently describes transaction facts as business events at the lowest useful granularity, generally with additive measurements where appropriate. ([Microsoft Learn][1])

---

# PART 39 — ADDITIVE VS SEMI-ADDITIVE

Now imagine:

```text
FactSales
```

has:

```text
quantity
net_amount
profit
```

These are generally additive.

You can use:

```text
SUM(net_amount)
```

across many dimensions.

But inventory:

```text
closing_quantity
```

is different.

You don't necessarily do:

```text
SUM(closing_quantity)
```

across time.

It is typically:

# SEMI-ADDITIVE

You may aggregate across:

```text
product
store
```

but not blindly across:

```text
date
```

This is why understanding measures is not merely:

> "numbers go in fact tables."

You need to understand **how those numbers can be aggregated**.

---

# PART 40 — NON-ADDITIVE

Examples:

```text
unit_price
margin_percent
conversion_rate
```

You don't generally:

```text
SUM(margin_percent)
```

Instead calculate the metric appropriately.

For example:

```text
profit / revenue
```

rather than summing individual percentages.

This is a major analytical correctness issue.

---

# PART 41 — FACT TABLE GRANULARITY

Here are GlobalMart's grains:

```text
FactSales
→ one product line in one order

FactPayment
→ one payment attempt

FactDelivery
→ one delivery

FactInventorySnapshot
→ one product + store + day

FactPurchaseOrder
→ one product line in one purchase order

FactGoodsReceipt
→ one product line received in one receipt event

FactReturn
→ one returned order line

FactSupportTicket
→ one support ticket

FactOrderFulfillment
→ one order lifecycle
```

Look at this.

Every fact has a **sentence** describing it.

That's what I want you to start doing.

---

# PART 42 — THE "ONE ROW =" TEST

For every fact you create, write:

```text
ONE ROW = __________________
```

If you can't say this clearly:

# DON'T CREATE THE FACT.

This single habit will eliminate a huge number of data-modeling mistakes.

---

# PART 43 — HOW TO DECIDE WHAT COLUMNS GO INTO THE FACT

Once the grain is defined:

### Ask:

> What dimensions are valid at this grain?

For FactSales:

```text
one order line
```

Therefore:

```text
customer
product
store
date
channel
promotion
```

may be valid.

Then:

> What measurements exist at this grain?

```text
quantity
price
discount
tax
cost
profit
```

Then:

> Do all these measures describe the same thing at the same grain?

If yes:

```text
FactSales
```

If no:

Split.

---

# PART 44 — HOW TO DECIDE WHAT BECOMES A DIMENSION

Do not say:

> "Anything that's text = dimension."

Wrong.

Instead ask:

> **Does this provide descriptive context that users will filter/group by?**

Examples:

```text
Customer
Product
Store
Supplier
Courier
Promotion
Date
Channel
Payment Method
```

These are natural dimensions.

---

# PART 45 — HOW TO DECIDE WHAT BECOMES A FACT

Ask:

> Is this a measurable event/state?

Examples:

```text
Sale
Payment
Return
Delivery
Inventory state
Purchase receipt
Support ticket
```

If yes, candidate fact.

But then you still need the grain decision.

---

# PART 46 — HOW TO DECIDE WHETHER TWO THINGS BELONG IN ONE FACT

Suppose:

```text
quantity
discount
tax
revenue
profit
```

All describe:

```text
one order line
```

→ Same fact.

But:

```text
payment_amount
delivery_minutes
inventory_balance
```

don't describe the same grain.

→ Separate facts.

The core test:

```text
Same process?
+
Same grain?
+
Measures naturally coexist?
```

If yes, same fact is often sensible.

---

# PART 47 — WHY NOT CREATE A FACT FOR EVERY QUESTION?

Because dimensions are designed to let one fact answer many analytical questions.

One FactSales can support:

```text
Revenue by country
Revenue by product
Revenue by customer
Revenue by category
Revenue by month
Revenue by channel
Revenue by store
Revenue by promotion
```

You don't need:

```text
FactRevenueCountry
FactRevenueProduct
FactRevenueCustomer
FactRevenueMonth
```

That's redundant.

---

# PART 48 — WHEN AN AGGREGATE FACT MAY MAKE SENSE

Suppose:

```text
FactSales
```

has:

```text billions of rows
```

and management constantly asks:

```text
daily revenue
by store
by category
```

You might create:

```text
FactSalesDailyStoreCategory
```

with grain:

# ONE ROW = ONE STORE + ONE PRODUCT CATEGORY + ONE DAY

This is not replacing the atomic fact.

It's an **aggregate** for a specific performance/use-case requirement.

Microsoft's current guidance recognizes aggregate fact tables as rollups of base facts used to accelerate common analytical queries. ([Microsoft Learn][1])

---

# PART 49 — DO NOT CONFUSE AGGREGATE WITH ATOMIC

Atomic:

```text
FactSales
one order line
```

Aggregate:

```text
FactSalesDailyStoreCategory
one store + category + day
```

The aggregate has less detail.

Therefore:

```text
Atomic → can produce aggregate
```

but:

```text
Aggregate → cannot recreate lost detail
```

This is why atomic grain is often preserved when practical.

---

# PART 50 — DIMENSION DESIGN

Dimensions aren't just lookup tables.

A good dimension supports:

```text
filtering
grouping
drill-down
labeling
historical interpretation
```

For example:

```text
DimProduct
```

might contain:

```text
department
category
subcategory
brand
product_type
```

That creates a hierarchy:

```text
Department
   ↓
Category
   ↓
Subcategory
   ↓
Product
```

Now analytics can drill:

```text
Department
→ Category
→ Subcategory
→ Product
```

---

# PART 51 — WHY DIMENSIONS CAN BE WIDE

A beginner may think:

> "A dimension should have only 5 columns."

No.

A useful dimension can be very wide.

Current Microsoft guidance explicitly notes that dimensions can be millions of rows and potentially hundreds of attributes; what matters is whether those attributes support required filtering, grouping, and accurate historical analysis. ([Microsoft Learn][6])

So:

```text
Dimension ≠ tiny table
```

---

# PART 52 — SNOWFLAKE DIMENSION

Suppose:

```text
DimProduct
```

contains:

```text
category
subcategory
department
```

You could normalize:

```text
DimProduct
    ↓
DimSubcategory
    ↓
DimCategory
    ↓
DimDepartment
```

That becomes a snowflake-style dimension.

But you should not automatically normalize everything.

For analytics, simpler dimension relationships can often make querying easier.

Microsoft currently recommends star-schema design for Fabric analytical workloads, while acknowledging that modeling is a balance rather than a rigid rule. ([Microsoft Learn][5])

---

# PART 53 — DATA MODEL VS PHYSICAL STORAGE

This is extremely important.

Suppose your logical design is:

```text
FactSales
DimCustomer
DimProduct
DimDate
```

You can physically implement it using:

```text
Fabric Warehouse
Snowflake
Databricks / Delta
BigQuery
Synapse
```

The logical model isn't the same thing as the physical platform.

Think:

```text
LOGICAL MODEL
What does the data mean?
        ↓
FACT / DIMENSION / GRAIN
        ↓
PHYSICAL PLATFORM
How do I store/process it?
```

This is why learning modeling concepts transfers across technologies.

---

# PART 54 — NOW PUT IT IN A MODERN LAKEHOUSE

Your modern GlobalMart architecture might be:

```text
                 SOURCE SYSTEMS
                       |
        ┌──────────────┼───────────────┐
        ↓              ↓               ↓
      OLTP            APIs           EVENTS
        └──────────────┼───────────────┘
                       ↓
                    BRONZE
                       ↓
                    SILVER
                       ↓
                     GOLD
                       ↓
      ┌────────────────┼───────────────────┐
      ↓                ↓                   ↓
  Sales Star      Inventory Star      Delivery Star
      ↓                ↓                   ↓
Power BI        ML / analytics       Applications
```

Databricks' current documentation describes Bronze → Silver → Gold as progressive refinement, with Gold used for business-ready data, and its current dimensional-modeling guidance says star schemas fit naturally into Gold. ([Databricks Documentation][7])

---

# PART 55 — WHAT SILVER DOES

Silver might contain:

```text
silver_orders
silver_order_lines
silver_customers
silver_products
silver_payments
silver_deliveries
silver_inventory
```

The data is:

```text
clean
validated
standardized
deduplicated
enriched
```

But Silver is usually not optimized around every business question.

---

# PART 56 — WHAT GOLD DOES

Gold might contain:

```text
FactSales
DimCustomer
DimProduct
DimStore
DimDate
DimPromotion

FactPayment
DimPaymentMethod

FactDelivery
DimCourier

FactInventorySnapshot
```

So Gold takes reusable Silver data and shapes it around business consumption.

Again:

> **Gold does not mean "one star only."**

It can contain many stars.

---

# PART 57 — IMPORTANT MODERN POINT

The architecture:

```text
Bronze → Silver → Gold
```

and the model:

```text
Fact + Dimension + Star
```

are **different layers of thinking**.

You can have:

```text
Lakehouse architecture
        +
Star-schema dimensional model
```

together.

This is exactly what current Databricks guidance demonstrates. ([Databricks Documentation][7])

---

# PART 58 — DATA QUALITY ENTERS AFTER MODELING

Suppose FactSales expects:

```text
customer_key
product_key
date_key
```

You need checks:

```text
customer key valid?
product key valid?
date key valid?
quantity valid?
amount valid?
duplicate order line?
```

Dimensions need checks:

```text
customer_id unique?
only one current SCD2 row?
category valid?
```

If a fact dimension lookup fails, a warehouse may use an **Unknown** dimension member rather than breaking the fact load; Microsoft explicitly describes this pattern and notes that ETL must still protect dimensional integrity. ([Microsoft Learn][9])

---

# PART 59 — LATE-ARRIVING DATA

Now advanced.

Suppose:

```text
Sale arrives today.
```

But customer information arrives tomorrow.

What happens?

You still may need to load the sale.

So the fact can initially point to:

```text
Unknown Customer
```

Then later be corrected when the dimension information arrives.

This is a **late-arriving dimension** problem.

This is the kind of thing you encounter in actual pipelines, not just textbook diagrams.

---

# PART 60 — SCD LOOKUP DURING FACT LOADING

Suppose FactSales contains:

```text
customer_key
```

The pipeline needs to determine:

> Which customer dimension version applies to this sale?

For SCD2:

```text
sale_date
      ↓
find customer version
      ↓
effective_from <= sale_date
AND
effective_to > sale_date
      ↓
retrieve customer_key
      ↓
write to FactSales
```

Current Microsoft guidance describes fact processing as looking up dimension surrogate keys, including the correct SCD2 version. ([Microsoft Learn][9])

That is very real Data Engineering work.

---

# PART 61 — WHAT IF THE SOURCE HAS DUPLICATES?

Suppose the source sends the same order line twice.

Your Silver pipeline may detect:

```text
duplicate business key
```

such as:

```text
order_id
+
order_line_number
```

You need idempotent processing.

This is why modeling, data quality, and pipeline engineering are connected.

---

# PART 62 — WHAT IF DATA ARRIVES TWICE?

A strong data pipeline should be able to handle retries.

You might use:

```text
business key
+
deduplication
+
MERGE / upsert
+
checkpointing
```

depending on platform and pipeline architecture.

The modeling decision matters because you need a stable way to identify the business event.

---

# PART 63 — WHAT IF AN ORDER IS CANCELLED?

Here is another modeling question.

Should you:

```text
delete the sale?
```

Usually you need to understand the business semantics first.

Perhaps:

```text
FactOrderLifecycle
```

tracks status changes.

Or:

```text
FactSales
```

contains the original sale and separate cancellation/return/refund facts record later business events.

The important part:

> **Don't let source CRUD operations automatically determine analytical semantics.**

Business events and analytical meaning come first.

---

# PART 64 — WHY BUSINESS REQUIREMENTS MATTER SO MUCH

Look at two companies.

### Company A

Only needs:

```text
daily revenue by store
```

Could be fairly simple.

### Company B

Needs:

```text
order-line profitability
payment attempts
inventory history
supplier performance
delivery SLA
returns
customer history
```

Same industry.

Completely different warehouse design.

Therefore:

> **There is no universal "correct fact table."**

The requirements determine the design.

Whenever you feel confused, ask:

### 2. What happened?

# PART 68 — THE BIGGEST THING TO AVOID

Never start with:

```text
"Here are my source tables.
I'll just convert each one into a fact/dimension."
```

That is **source-driven modeling**.

Instead:

```text
Business requirements
        ↓
business processes
        ↓
analytical model
        ↓
source mapping
```

That's much stronger.

# PART 69 — SOURCE TABLE DOES NOT EQUAL FACT TABLE

For example, source database may contain:

```text
orders
order_items
payments
shipments
inventory_transactions
customers
products
```

You may create:

```text
FactSales
FactPayment
FactDelivery
FactInventorySnapshot
```

Notice:

```text
inventory_transactions
```

doesn't necessarily become:

```text
FactInventorySnapshot
```

You may transform many inventory events into a daily snapshot.

That's a huge insight.

> **Fact tables are analytical representations of business processes, not necessarily copies of source tables.**

# PART 70 — GOLD MODEL DOES NOT HAVE TO MIRROR SILVER

Silver:

```text
orders
order_items
payment_attempts
inventory_movements
```

Gold:

```text
FactSales
FactPayment
FactInventorySnapshot
```

Different structure.

Different purpose.

That is completely normal.

# PART 71 — HOW TO CHECK WHETHER YOUR MODEL MAKES SENSE

After designing the model, test business questions.

For example:

### Question

> Revenue by country and month.

Can we do it?

```text
FactSales
→ DimCustomer
→ DimDate
```

Yes.

### Question

> Payment failure rate by payment method.

```text
FactPayment
→ DimPaymentMethod
```

Yes.

### Question

> Average delivery time by store.

```text
FactDelivery
→ DimStore
```

Yes.

### Question

> Daily closing inventory by product and store.

```text
FactInventorySnapshot
→ DimProduct
→ DimStore
→ DimDate
```

Yes.

### Question

> Historical customer segment at time of sale.

```text
FactSales
→ SCD2 DimCustomer
```

Yes.

Now you've validated the model.

# PART 72 — "CAN MY MODEL ANSWER THE REQUIREMENTS?"

This is one of your final design checks.

Make a matrix:

| Requirement            | Fact                   | Dimensions                   |
| ---------------------- | ---------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| Revenue by country     | FactSales              | Customer, Date               |
| Revenue by product     | FactSales              | Product                      |
| Payment failure        | FactPayment            | Payment Method, Date         |
| Delivery SLA           | FactDelivery           | Store, Courier, Date         |
| Daily stock            | FactInventorySnapshot  | Product, Store, Date         |

| Return rate            | FactReturn + FactSales | Product, Customer, Date      |
| Support resolution     | FactSupportTicket      | Agent, Issue, Customer       |

If a requirement cannot be answered cleanly, revisit your model.

# PART 73 — THE "WHY" OF STAR SCHEMA

Now you should see why we create it.

Not because:

> "The textbook says fact goes in the center."

We create it because analytics usually asks:

```text
MEASURE
BY
DIMENSIONS
```

Example:

```text
SUM(net_amount)
BY
country
category
month
```

Fact:

```text
net_amount
```

Dimensions:

```text
country
category
month
```

That matches the way analytical questions are asked.

Microsoft's current guidance describes this exact relationship: dimensions support filtering/grouping while facts support summarization. ([Microsoft Learn][8])
